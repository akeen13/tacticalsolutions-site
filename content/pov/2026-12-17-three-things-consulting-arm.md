> We have not run a Loop Pilot yet. Nobody has signed one. That is exactly why this is worth writing now, while the shape is still a decision rather than a story we tell after the fact.

We are standing up the consulting arm of Tactical Solutions: Tactical Solutions Applied.

A Loop Pilot is fixed fee, 90 days, one operational loop closed to production inside a customer's stack, owned by the customer's team at the end. Not a strategy deck. Not a proof of concept. A working autonomous workflow handed over, with the customer's name on the runbook rather than ours.

Before we sign the first one, I want the three principles public. Two reasons. It forces our hand, because once it is written down we have to hold it. And if you are an operator weighing us up, you should know the constraints before you get on a call, not after.

**Principle 1: Handoff is the work, not the build.**

The thing that goes wrong on autonomous-system handoffs is always the same one. The team that built it cannot hand it over cleanly, and the team receiving it cannot run it without the builders on a rope forever. So the handoff gets designed from day one.

Concretely: every Loop Pilot ships with a runbook, short and blunt, that treats the operator on the receiving end as the adult they are. Written in week one, not week twelve. Rewritten every fortnight against what is actually happening. By cutover the runbook is the system's manual and the customer's team has edited parts of it themselves. If they cannot, we have not shipped.

That makes the runbook a deliverable, not documentation. It also means the build cannot outrun the handoff, because the handoff is being written while the build happens.

**Principle 2: One named human per loop. No committees.**

Every loop gets exactly one person on the customer side with authority to pull the ripcord. Not a shared inbox. Not a steering group. One name, one number, and the standing authority to stop the thing without asking anybody first.

This is non-negotiable at scoping. If a prospect cannot name that person before we sign, we do not sign. The pattern is too predictable: distributed ownership at the start is an unowned system by month four, and an unowned autonomous system in production is a liability wearing a progress report.

It is also unpopular. Committees are how mid-size firms avoid putting one person's name on a decision. Naming somebody means somebody can be wrong out loud. That is the point.

**Principle 3: One loop, fully shipped. Not three loops, half-shipped.**

The default consulting failure is overscoping at sale and undershipping at delivery: the three-loop pilot that produces two demos and a prototype nobody runs.

The SOW caps it at one loop into production. Cutover is the deliverable. Expansion to a second loop is its own engagement, priced and signed separately, scoped with the day-zero knowledge that only exists once the first loop is running in real conditions.

The arithmetic is simple. One loop fully owned by the customer in 90 days beats three loops half-owned. Customers who push back on the cap in the first call are usually the ones who would have been hurt most by the overscope. Losing that deal is cheaper than shipping the wrong shape.

**What this costs us and what it buys you.**

The costs are real. Narrow scope loses deals to firms promising a transformation programme. Naming one human rules out the prospects whose sign-off is committee-shaped. Publishing the plan means being held to it.

What it buys you is a fixed number and a fixed end date. The Loop Pilot is $150,000, fixed fee, 90 days, one loop, one measurable outcome. No day-rate creep, no change-order theatre, and a cutover date that does not move because somebody found more scope.

The first cohort is the one place that number moves. Take a slot before we have a named case study and we will discount the Pilot for the right to publish it. You get the work cheaper, we get the reference. After that, the price is the price.

If that number is wrong for your operation, a two-week paid scoping sprint exists at $25,000, credited against the Pilot if you go ahead, and you keep the written scope either way. If the loop is not ready to run autonomously, we say so and you keep the document.

**What happens if it fails.**

Worth naming, because every vendor's deck implies it will not. If we hit day 90 without a loop in production, you keep everything: the operator, the runbook, the audit trail, the scope. The fee is the fee and it does not come back, but nothing else does either. There is no licence to keep paying and no support contract that quietly becomes mandatory. The point of a handoff is that you do not need us afterwards, and a consulting arm that needs to be needed has not done the job.

Three principles. One loop, one human, one handoff, in 90 days, at a fixed price. That is the whole offer.
